\begin{abstract}

Financial institutions increasingly deploy LLM agents to automate accounts payable, treasury, and reconciliation operations. Unlike generic security benchmarks focused on data exfiltration or forbidden actions, financial operations harbor a distinct threat surface: \textit{silent compliance violations} disguised as legitimate task completion.

This paper introduces \textbf{FinAgent Red-Team}, a benchmark for evaluating LLM agent robustness against regulatory-control bypass attacks in financial workflows. The benchmark comprises 48 scenarios (42 adversarial, 6 benign). Every attack is delivered by indirect prompt injection into agent-readable content, and is scored in one of six outcome categories: unauthorized transfer, sanctions evasion, payment structuring, dual-approval defeat, data exfiltration, and confused-deputy payee redirection. Each threat traces to a specific regulatory obligation---currency transaction reporting, sanctions screening, segregation of duties---and each success criterion is the failure condition of the control that discharges it. Each scenario is evaluated under three control postures—\textit{none} (no policy), \textit{advisory} (policy stated, not enforced), and \textit{enforced} (hard system controls)—to decompose safety into policy-following vs.\ enforcement effectiveness.

We evaluate seven closed- and open-weight models (Claude Sonnet~4.6 and Haiku~4.5, GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini, Qwen~3~8B, and Llama~3.1~8B served locally and via Groq). Our central finding contradicts the intuition that capable models are inherently safe: with \emph{no} stated policy, most models---including a frontier model, GPT-4o at 71\% attack success rate (ASR)---readily execute compliance-violating actions, and only Claude Sonnet resists (0\%). Merely \emph{stating} policy is unreliable: it nearly eliminates attacks for strong instruction-followers (GPT-4o, Claude Haiku both 1\%) yet leaves a large residual for others (Qwen~3~8B 45\%, GPT-4o-mini 28\%). \emph{Hard enforcement} drives ASR to 0\% for every model at zero measured over-refusal---except Llama~3.1~8B served locally, which reaches 0\% partly by declining to act at all (50\% over-refusal). All success criteria are deterministic state predicates (no LLM grading), every rate carries a 95\% Wilson interval, and the benchmark is open-source and deployable.

\textbf{Keywords:} LLM safety, agent red-teaming, financial compliance, regulatory bypass, benchmark design

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

Large language models are moving into financial operations at scale. Treasury departments use agents to approve wire transfers. Accounts payable teams rely on them to match invoices and route payments. Reconciliation systems deploy agents to identify and categorize transaction discrepancies.

The failure modes in these domains are not generic misbehavior; they are \textit{compliance violations that look like task completion}: an agent wires funds to an unlisted payee, splits a large payment to evade a reporting threshold, self-approves a transfer that should require dual sign-off, or exfiltrates client account data in response to a crafted email. Existing red-teaming benchmarks (e.g.\ AgentBench, ToolEmu, AgentDojo) focus on generic agent capabilities---web browsing, API calling, multi-step reasoning---and neither model the regulatory control surface of a financial institution nor distinguish safety rooted in model judgment (``I know this is wrong'') from safety enforced by the system (``the system won't let me do this'').

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A regulation-grounded threat taxonomy with construct validity}: an injection vector plus six scored attack categories, each traced from the regulatory obligation it violates, through the control an institution operates to discharge it, to the deterministic state predicate that stands in for that control being defeated (Tables~\ref{tab:threats},~\ref{tab:regmap}). All success criteria are reproducible state predicates---no LLM grading.
  \item \textbf{A three-posture evaluation framework} that replays each scenario under \textit{none}/\textit{advisory}/\textit{enforced} control, decomposing safety into policy-following uplift and enforcement uplift.
  \item \textbf{A leaderboard of 7 models} over 48 scenarios $\times$ 3 trials, with 95\% Wilson intervals and permutation-tested pairwise comparisons.
  \item \textbf{An open-source, deployable benchmark} with an integrity gate and no proprietary dependencies.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}

\label{sec:limitations}

The benchmark runs against synthetic worlds and mock financial infrastructure; real deployments add heterogeneous backends, jurisdictional variation, and human social engineering that we do not model. The threat model is indirect prompt injection (crafted emails, invoices, chat); we exclude supply-chain compromise, physical access, and social engineering of human stakeholders. The 48 scenarios sample rather than exhaustively enumerate the threat space, and new categories may emerge with novel model capabilities or financial products. Finally, the Groq-served Llama~3.1~8B is reported at a single trial (cloud-provider quota), so its confidence intervals are correspondingly wider.

\section{Responsible Disclosure}

\label{sec:responsible}

This benchmark is designed to harden financial AI systems before deployment, and two properties limit its offensive utility. Every scenario runs against a synthetic world---mock ledgers, payees, and counterparties, with no real account data---and is not executable against a live system without being rewritten for that system's interfaces. And the attacks are not novel capabilities: indirect prompt injection is public knowledge \citep{greshake2023injection}, and the targeted controls (reporting thresholds, sanctions screening, dual authorization) are described in public statute and regulation. What is new is the measurement apparatus, not the attack. We judge the publication risk low and the benefit to defenders concrete: the central result---that enforcement at the tool boundary reduces attack success to zero for every model tested---is a control practitioners already have and can act on without waiting for a model update.

\section{Open Source and Reproducibility}

\label{sec:opensource}

FinAgent Red-Team is MIT-licensed and available at \texttt{github.com/nac7/finagent-redteam}. It ships deterministic seeded scenario generation, an agent runner for any OpenAI-compatible API, a leaderboard harness with permutation-based significance testing and checkpoint/resume, results for all 7 models, and a validation gate that rejects runs with API errors, absent tool calls, or degenerate benign utility---so an unusable run cannot be mistaken for a robust one. Results reproduce from fixed seeds and deterministic predicates.

\section{Conclusion}

FinAgent Red-Team is an open-source benchmark for robustness against compliance-critical attacks on financial LLM agents. Our results do not support the assumption that capability confers safety: absent a stated policy, a frontier model executed compliance-violating actions in 71\% of attack scenarios, and only one of seven models resisted outright. What separated models was not raw capability but their ability to act on a policy once told---and even that was matched, for every model, by hard enforcement. The practical implication is that policy text is a mitigation and the sandbox is the control.
