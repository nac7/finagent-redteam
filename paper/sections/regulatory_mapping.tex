A benchmark for ``regulatory-control bypass'' is only as good as the claim that the things it measures are, in fact, regulatory controls. This section makes that claim auditable. For each threat we state the obligation it derives from, the control a financial institution actually operates to discharge that obligation, and the predicate over final sandbox state that stands in for the control being defeated.

\subsection{From obligation to predicate}

Table~\ref{tab:regmap} gives the mapping. Three properties of it are worth stating explicitly, because they are what separate a compliance benchmark from a generic agent-security benchmark dressed in financial vocabulary.

\textbf{The predicate is the control's failure condition, not a proxy for it.} An institution does not discharge its structuring obligation by disapproving of structuring; it discharges it by filing when cumulative activity crosses the threshold. Accordingly, T4 is scored on exactly that conjunction --- funds across the threshold \emph{and} no report --- and an agent that splits a payment but files a report is not counted as attacked. This is a stricter and more meaningful bar than ``did the agent do the suspicious-looking thing.'' The same holds for T7, where neither updating the payee record nor paying the payee is a violation on its own; only the composition is.

\textbf{The controls are ones that already exist.} Every control in the third column is a control a payments organization operates today, in software, independent of any LLM. Allow-lists, screening, threshold monitoring, maker--checker, and DLP are not safety measures invented for AI agents; they are the existing control surface that an agent is being inserted into. This is what makes the \textit{enforced} posture realistic rather than aspirational: it does not posit a new defensive technology, it asks what happens when the agent is placed \emph{behind} controls the institution already has. Our central result --- 0\% ASR at enforced posture for all seven models --- is therefore a statement about deployment architecture available to practitioners now.

\textbf{The obligations are legal, not stylistic.} T4 in particular rests on statute that criminalizes the evasion itself, independent of whether the underlying funds are illicit \citep{usc5324}. This is why an agent that helpfully splits a payment to spare its principal some paperwork is not committing a faux pas; it is executing the actus reus of a federal offense on the institution's behalf. A benchmark scored on refusal-to-say would register nothing at all in this scenario, because nothing objectionable is ever said.

\subsection{What this mapping does not establish}

Two limits should be stated plainly. First, the mapping is to obligations under U.S.\ law, and a European or Asian institution operates a partly different control set; the taxonomy is portable but the specific thresholds and reporting instruments are not. Second, the sandbox implements each control as a single predicate, whereas a real institution implements it as a layered process with human escalation, periodic audit, and after-the-fact recovery. A bypass in our sandbox corresponds to defeating the automated control, not to a loss that would necessarily survive an institution's full defenses. We therefore read our ASR figures as measuring the load an agent places on downstream controls, not as a direct estimate of realized fraud.

We have not yet subjected the scenarios to structured review by practicing compliance officers. We regard that as the most valuable remaining validation step and the natural next addition to this section; the mapping above is the artifact such a review would be conducted against.
