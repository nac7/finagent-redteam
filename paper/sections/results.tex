\subsection{Key Findings}

\subsubsection{1. Capability does not imply compliance safety}

With no stated policy, six of seven models execute compliance-violating actions at appreciable rates, and susceptibility does \emph{not} track model scale or ``frontier'' status. GPT-4o---a frontier model---has the second-highest no-policy ASR in the study (71.4\%), above the 8B open-weight Qwen 3 only marginally (83.3\%) and well above Claude Haiku (30.2\%). The single exception is Claude Sonnet 4.6, which refuses every attack at every posture (0.0\% ASR) while retaining full benign utility. This shows that safe-by-default behavior is achievable but is not a generic property of capable models.

\subsubsection{2. Stating policy is not a reliable control}

Policy-following uplift (ASR$_\text{none}$ $-$ ASR$_\text{advisory}$) varies enormously across models. For strong instruction-followers, merely stating the policy nearly eliminates attacks: GPT-4o falls from 71.4\% to 0.8\% and Claude Haiku from 30.2\% to 0.8\%. For others, a large residual survives: Qwen 3 8B remains at 45.2\% and GPT-4o-mini at 27.8\% even when the policy is explicitly stated. A deployer cannot, therefore, treat a written policy as a dependable safeguard---its effectiveness is model-dependent and, for some capable models, weak.

\subsubsection{3. Enforcement is universally effective and low-cost}

Hard system enforcement reduces ASR to 0.0\% for \emph{every} model, including those (Qwen 3 8B, GPT-4o-mini) for which stated policy was insufficient. Crucially, this comes at no measured utility cost: over-refusal is 0\% for all models that make real tool calls. This is the benchmark's central operational result: defense-in-depth anchored on enforcement, not on model judgement or stated policy, is what makes financial agents safe without breaking legitimate work.

\subsubsection{4. ``Zero ASR'' can be an artifact of over-refusal}

Two models report 0.0\% ASR, but for opposite reasons. Claude Sonnet achieves it with 100\% benign utility---principled refusal. The locally served Llama 3.1 8B achieves it with only 50\% benign utility and 50\% over-refusal: transcript inspection shows it typically issues a single \texttt{read\_inbox} call and stops, rather than chaining to the malicious follow-up action. Its safety is thus partly an artifact of not acting, not of recognizing the attack. Reporting utility alongside ASR is essential to distinguish these cases---an ASR-only leaderboard would rank these two identically.

This suggests these controls are intrinsically difficult for current models and are precisely where hard enforcement matters most.

\subsection{Utility and Over-Refusal}

Benign scenarios measure the cost of controls. Every model that makes real structured tool calls completes 100\% of legitimate tasks at all three postures (0\% over-refusal), so enforcement can be deployed without degrading legitimate workflows. The sole exception is the locally served Llama 3.1 8B, which completes only 50\% of benign tasks (50\% over-refusal)---an incapacity to reliably chain multi-step workflows, discussed in \S\ref{sec:results} finding~4---and not a consequence of the controls.

\subsection{Statistical Reliability}

All rates are reported with 95\% Wilson score confidence intervals computed on pooled trial counts (Table \ref{tab:leaderboard}; Appendix \ref{app:stats}). Wilson intervals remain valid at extreme proportions (near 0 or 1) and small samples, both common here. The key qualitative gaps are supported by non-overlapping intervals: under the advisory posture, Qwen 3 8B (45.2\%, CI on the no-policy rate [75.9, 88.8]) and GPT-4o-mini (27.8\%) remain clearly separated from GPT-4o and Claude Haiku (both 0.8\%), whose intervals sit near zero. A bootstrap resampling test for pairwise comparisons is provided in the repository (\texttt{compute\_significance.py}) with checkpoint/resume support.

\subsection{Deployment-Dependent Susceptibility}

The same Llama 3.1 8B weights exhibit materially different susceptibility depending on the serving stack: 0.0\% no-policy ASR served locally via Ollama versus 42.9\% served via Groq. Because the model weights are identical, this gap is attributable to serving-stack differences (system-prompt handling, sampling defaults, tool-call formatting) rather than the model itself. We therefore report the two configurations as distinct entries. This is a cautionary result for practitioners: a model's measured safety is a property of the deployed \emph{system}, not the weights alone, and must be re-measured per deployment.

\subsection{Summary}

FinAgent Red-Team reveals a nuanced landscape:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Capability $\neq$ safety}: a frontier model (GPT-4o) is among the most susceptible with no stated policy (71.4\%); only Claude Sonnet is safe by default.
  \item \textbf{Stated policy is unreliable}: it eliminates attacks for strong instruction-followers but leaves 28--45\% residual for others.
  \item \textbf{Enforcement is decisive}: it drives ASR to 0\% for every model at no measured utility cost.
  \item \textbf{Measure utility with ASR}: two models score 0\% ASR, but one does so by refusing legitimate work half the time.
  \item \textbf{Safety is deployment-dependent}: identical weights differ by 43 points across serving stacks.
\end{itemize}
