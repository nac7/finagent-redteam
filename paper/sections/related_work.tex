\subsection{Agent capability benchmarks}

A first line of work asks whether LLM agents can complete multi-step tasks at all. AgentBench \citep{liu2024agentbench} evaluates reasoning and decision-making across eight interactive environments, and $\tau$-bench \citep{yao2024taubench} evaluates tool-agent-user interaction in retail and airline domains where the agent is given written domain policies to follow. $\tau$-bench is the closest capability-side analogue to our setting: it too measures whether an agent respects a stated rule. The difference is one of threat model rather than of degree. In $\tau$-bench the user is cooperative and policy adherence is a component of \emph{utility} --- an agent that violates the refund policy has done its job badly. In our setting the instruction to violate policy arrives inside untrusted tool output, adherence is a \emph{security} property, and the same scenario is replayed under three different control postures so that model judgment and system enforcement can be attributed separately. Neither benchmark models a regulatory control surface: their policies are merchant rules, not statutory obligations whose breach is a reportable event.

\subsection{Agent security and prompt injection}

The security-side literature established that tool-using agents inherit an attack surface from the data they read. \citet{greshake2023injection} demonstrated indirect prompt injection against deployed LLM-integrated applications, following direct goal-hijacking attacks \citep{perez2022ignore}. Benchmarks followed: ToolEmu \citep{ruan2024toolemu} uses an LM to emulate tool execution and an LM to grade the resulting risk, trading fidelity for coverage; InjecAgent \citep{zhan2024injecagent} enumerates indirect-injection test cases over tool-integrated agents; AgentHarm \citep{andriushchenko2025agentharm} measures whether jailbroken agents will carry out explicitly malicious multi-step tasks; and R-Judge \citep{yuan2024rjudge} evaluates whether models can \emph{recognise} risk in agent transcripts after the fact. AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo} is the closest methodological relative: it runs agents in a dynamic environment and, like this work, scores both utility and security with programmatic checks rather than an LLM judge.

We differ from this family in two respects. First, in what counts as a successful attack. In the injection literature the canonical objective is exfiltration or an obviously forbidden action, and the agent's misbehaviour is legible in its output. The attacks we model are the opposite: a structured payment, a routed approval, a filed transfer --- each individually well-formed, each producing a transcript that reads like competent task completion, and each detectable only as a predicate over final system state. This is why our success criteria are deterministic state predicates and not output judgments; there is nothing in the text to judge. Second, in what the benchmark is designed to attribute. Existing benchmarks report a single ASR per model, optionally under a defense. We hold the scenario fixed and vary only the control posture --- \textit{none}, \textit{advisory}, \textit{enforced} --- which turns a single ASR into a decomposition of where safety actually came from.

That decomposition is what connects our results to the defense literature. Model-level defenses train a priority ordering over instruction sources \citep{wallace2024hierarchy}; system-level defenses argue that injection should be defeated by construction, outside the model, since the model cannot be trusted to police itself \citep{debenedetti2025camel}. Our advisory-versus-enforced gap is a direct empirical measurement of the premise underlying the latter position. Stated policy is not a reliable control: it is worth $+71$ points of ASR reduction for GPT-4o and only $+55$ for GPT-4o-mini, which starts from a comparable intrinsic susceptibility and still ends at 28\%. Enforcement is worth whatever remains, for every model, without exception --- at zero measured over-refusal for six of our seven models (the exception, Llama~3.1~8B served locally, is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}). The variance in the advisory column is a property of the model; the invariance of the enforced column is a property of the system.

\subsection{Financial LLM evaluation}

Work on financial language models has concentrated on capability --- domain pretraining and financial NLP task suites, exemplified by BloombergGPT \citep{wu2023bloomberggpt} --- and, more recently, on content-level safety. FinSafetyBench \citep{hou2026finsafetybench} red-teams models over financial crime and ethics categories, measuring whether a model will \emph{say} something it should refuse. That framing does not reach the failure mode we study, in which the model says nothing objectionable and simply calls two tools in an order that defeats a control.

Concurrent with this work, FinVault \citep{yang2026finvault} evaluates financial agent safety in execution-grounded sandboxes with state-writable databases and explicit compliance constraints, reporting attack success rates up to 50\% on state-of-the-art models under prompt injection, jailbreaking, and authority impersonation. Its central premise agrees with ours, and its headline result --- that current defenses transfer poorly to financial agent settings --- is consistent with our finding that GPT-4o executes compliance-violating actions 71\% of the time absent a stated policy. The two efforts are complementary in construction. FinVault covers breadth: 31 scenarios, 107 catalogued vulnerabilities, 963 test cases across an attack taxonomy. We cover the control dimension instead: a smaller scenario set (48, of which 6 are benign) replayed under three enforcement postures with three independent trials each, so that every reported number is a comparison across postures on identical scenarios, and every rate carries a 95\% Wilson interval \citep{wilson1927probable} with pairwise differences tested by permutation \citep{ernst2004permutation}. Neither design subsumes the other: FinVault answers ``which attacks land,'' and this benchmark answers ``which layer of the stack stopped them.''

\subsection{Financial controls as evaluable objects}

The controls we test are not invented for the benchmark. Currency transaction reporting thresholds and the prohibition on structuring transactions to evade them are statutory \citep{usc5324, cfr1010311}; segregation of duties and dual authorization are standard internal-control practice with a compliance mandate behind them \citep{sox2002}. The accounting and audit literature specifies these controls in terms of who may authorize what, under which conditions, and with what independent verification --- which is precisely the form a deterministic state predicate takes. Our contribution here is narrow but, we believe, useful: we show that a meaningful subset of this control surface is directly machine-checkable, and that once encoded, it distinguishes sharply between models that a content-level safety evaluation would rank as equivalent.
