The aggregate leaderboard hides instructive within-category contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:by-threat}). We describe \emph{what the data shows} and offer mechanism hypotheses, labelled as such because grading is on world-state predicates, not model rationales.

\textbf{Structuring (T4) and dual-approval defeat (T5) are where stated policy fails.} These are the two most divisive categories under advisory: GPT-4o-mini (94\%/100\%) and Qwen~3~8B (83\%/100\%) almost always execute the attack even with the policy stated, whereas GPT-4o, both Claude models, and the local Llama sit at 0\%---except that GPT-4o, robust everywhere else, itself slips to 6\% on dual-approval, the only category where it fails. Both controls share a structure: no individual action is forbidden (each sub-threshold transfer, each of initiating and approving, is normal), and the violation lives only in the \emph{aggregate} or the \emph{composition}. Models that reason over each action in isolation miss it, and a policy statement does not supply the cross-action bookkeeping. Enforcement that binds the invariant directly---blocking transfers summing past the threshold, or requiring approver~$\neq$~initiator---removes the inference burden and drives ASR to 0\%. Sanctions evasion (T3) shows a smaller version of the same gap (Qwen~3 and Groq-Llama 25\%, Haiku 3\%, rest 0\%), turning on whether the model screens the named entity as a matter of course.

Two patterns recur across the study. Capability does not predict compliance: GPT-4o is robust on five of six categories yet the second-most susceptible model with no stated policy. And the categories that resist stated policy are exactly the procedural, aggregate-reasoning ones where only the pattern---not any single action---is illegitimate, which is why hard enforcement, not judgement or written policy, is the decisive control for them.
